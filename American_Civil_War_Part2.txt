Then, in June 1862, the Confederacy got a new commander for its main army in Virginia. Robert E. Lee.

Lee was bold, aggressive and very good at reading his opponents. Within weeks, he had pushed McClellan's army away from the Confederate capital, Richmond. And then he did something daring: he invaded the North.

The two armies met near a creek called Antietam, in Maryland.

The seventeenth of September, 1862, is still the bloodiest single day in American history. In about twelve hours, roughly twenty-three thousand men were killed, wounded or missing. One cornfield changed hands so many times that, by the end, every stalk had been cut down by bullets.

Lee's army was battered, and it retreated. McClellan, of course, didn't chase it.

But Lincoln had been waiting for a victory. Any victory. And this was close enough.

Five days later, he announced the Emancipation Proclamation. Starting on the first of January, 1863, every enslaved person in the rebelling states would be, in the words of the document, "thenceforward, and forever free."

Now, it had limits. It only applied to areas in rebellion, places Lincoln didn't actually control yet. But it changed the meaning of the war completely.

Before, the North was fighting to put the country back together. Now, it was fighting to end slavery. And every mile the Union army marched forward, freedom marched with it.

It also had a clever side effect. Britain and France had been thinking about supporting the Confederacy. But now, supporting the South meant openly supporting slavery, and the British public would never accept that.

The South had lost its best hope of help from abroad.

Lee kept winning anyway.

At Fredericksburg in December 1862, Union soldiers charged up a hill into a stone wall and were cut down in waves. At Chancellorsville in May 1863, Lee, outnumbered more than two to one, split his army in half and still won. It's often called his masterpiece.

But it came at a price. Riding back through the woods after dark, Stonewall Jackson was shot by mistake. By his own men.

He died a week later. Lee said he had lost his right arm.

Lee decided to roll the dice one more time. He marched north again, into Pennsylvania. One big victory on Northern soil, he hoped, might make the North finally give up.

The two armies crashed into each other at a quiet little town that nobody had planned to fight in.

For three days, they fought over the hills and fields around Gettysburg.

On the third day, Lee ordered one final, enormous attack straight into the centre of the Union line. About twelve thousand men marched across almost a mile of open ground, under heavy fire the whole way.

It's known as Pickett's Charge. And it was a disaster. More than half of the men who took part were killed, wounded or captured.

When the survivors stumbled back, Lee rode out to meet them and said: "It's all my fault."

Lee never invaded the North again.

And the very next day, on the fourth of July, America's birthday, eight hundred miles away, something just as big happened.

After a siege of more than six weeks, the city of Vicksburg, the Confederacy's last great fortress on the Mississippi River, surrendered to Grant.

The Union now controlled the entire Mississippi. The Confederacy was sliced in half. The anaconda had closed one coil.

In just two days, the war had turned.

That November, Lincoln travelled to Gettysburg to help open a cemetery for the soldiers who had died there.

The main speaker that day talked for about two hours. Then Lincoln stood up and spoke for about two minutes. Just two hundred and seventy-two words.

He said the war was a test of whether a nation "conceived in liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal" could survive at all. And he promised that this nation would have "a new birth of freedom," and that "government of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not perish from the earth."

Lincoln himself said the world would "little note, nor long remember" what was said there.

He was wrong about that.

The Emancipation Proclamation also opened the army to Black men, and they signed up in huge numbers. By the end of the war, around one hundred and eighty thousand Black soldiers had served in the Union army, many of them men who had escaped slavery themselves.

They were paid less than white soldiers and faced death or re-enslavement if they were captured. They fought anyway. The 54th Massachusetts became famous for leading a near-hopeless attack on Fort Wagner in South Carolina, proving to a doubtful North exactly what they were willing to give for this country.

But not everyone in the North was united. When the government started drafting men into the army, the rich could pay three hundred dollars to get out of it. In New York City, furious crowds rioted for days, and some of the worst violence was aimed at the city's Black residents.

Even in the North, the war exposed old wounds.

By 1864, Lincoln had finally found a general who would fight. He brought Grant east and put him in charge of all the Union armies.

Grant's plan was simple and brutal: attack everywhere at once, and never let up.

He went straight at Lee. At the Wilderness, a forest so thick the soldiers could barely see each other, the woods caught fire around the wounded. At Cold Harbor, thousands of Union soldiers fell in a single morning attack. Before going in, many of them pinned their names to their coats, so their bodies could be identified.

Earlier generals, after a beating like that, would have retreated. Grant didn't. He just slid sideways and kept moving south.

Eventually, the two armies dug in around the city of Petersburg, just south of Richmond. Miles and miles of trenches. Mud, cannon fire, and waiting, for nine months.

It looked a lot like the trench warfare of World War One. Fifty years early.

Meanwhile, in the West, Grant's close friend William Tecumseh Sherman was driving deep into Georgia.

In September 1864, Sherman captured Atlanta, a key railway hub of the South.

And the timing could not have been better for Lincoln. There was an election that November, and the North was exhausted. Lincoln himself expected to lose to a candidate who might make peace with the South.

The fall of Atlanta changed everything. The North believed it could win again. Lincoln was re-elected.

Then Sherman did something shocking. He cut himself off from his supply lines and marched his army three hundred miles across Georgia to the sea, living off the land and destroying railways, factories, bridges and crops along the way.

He believed the only way to end the war was to break the South's ability, and will, to keep fighting. He said: "War is cruelty, and you cannot refine it."

Just before Christmas, Sherman reached the coastal city of Savannah, and sent Lincoln a telegram offering him the city as a Christmas present.

As the war entered its final months, Lincoln knew the Emancipation Proclamation was a wartime order. It could be challenged later. So he pushed for something permanent.

On the thirty-first of January, 1865, the House of Representatives passed the Thirteenth Amendment to the Constitution, abolishing slavery across the entire United States. When it was ratified later that year, it became the law of the land.

After nearly two and a half centuries, slavery in America was over.

On the battlefield, Lee's army was starving and shrinking. In early April, the line at Petersburg finally broke. Lee abandoned Richmond, and the Confederate capital fell.

Days later, Lincoln walked through the streets of Richmond himself. Freed people crowded around him, some falling to their knees. Lincoln told them not to kneel to him. They should kneel only to God.

Lee tried to escape west, but Grant's army got ahead of him. Trapped and out of food, Lee knew it was over.

On the ninth of April, 1865, the two generals met in a farmhouse in the village of Appomattox Court House.

Lee arrived in a spotless dress uniform. Grant showed up in a muddy private's coat.

And Grant was generous. Lee's soldiers could go home. They could keep their horses for spring planting. Grant even sent food to Lee's starving men. When Union soldiers started firing cannons to celebrate, Grant ordered them to stop. The rebels, he said, were our countrymen again.

Oh, and here's a fun fact.

The farmhouse belonged to a man named Wilmer McLean. And Wilmer McLean had once lived near Bull Run, where the first big battle of the war was fought. In fact, a cannonball crashed through his kitchen there.

So he moved his family a hundred miles away, somewhere quiet, to get away from the war.

The war, as they say, began in his kitchen and ended in his parlour.

Five days after the surrender, on Good Friday evening, Abraham Lincoln and his wife went to Ford's Theatre in Washington to watch a comedy.

During the play, an actor and Confederate supporter named John Wilkes Booth crept into the president's box and shot Lincoln in the back of the head.

Lincoln died the next morning.

His funeral train travelled slowly across the country, back to his home in Illinois. Along the route, millions of people stood beside the tracks to watch it pass.

The man who had held the country together did not live to see it healed.

Over four years, around six hundred and twenty thousand soldiers died. Some historians now believe the real number was closer to three-quarters of a million. Two out of every three of them died not from bullets but from disease.

Out of a population of around thirty-one million, almost every family in America had lost someone.

And the news of freedom travelled slowly. It was not until the nineteenth of June, 1865, that Union soldiers arrived in Galveston, Texas, and announced that the enslaved people there were free.

That day is still celebrated as Juneteenth, and today it's a national holiday.

In the years that followed, a period called Reconstruction, the country tried to rebuild the South and protect the newly freed. Two more amendments followed. The Fourteenth promised citizenship and equal protection of the law to everyone born in the United States. The Fifteenth said no one could be denied the vote because of their race.

But many of those promises would be broken. When Reconstruction ended, the South found new ways to keep Black Americans from voting and to keep them separate and unequal. The fight for real equality would take another hundred years, and in many ways, it's still going on.

So, what did the Civil War actually do?

It settled two questions that America had been dodging since the day it was born.

First: could a nation that said "all men are created equal" also hold four million people in chains? The answer, paid for in blood, was no.

And second: could a state simply walk out of the United States whenever it didn't like an election result? The answer to that was also no.

People like to say that before the war, Americans tended to talk about the United States as a group of states. "The United States are." And after the war, more and more, they talked about one single nation.

"The United States is."

One word. Six hundred thousand lives.

And a country that, for the first time, had to start trying to live up to the words it was founded on.
